Why Events, Not Lambda Chains

The coordination problem that EventBridge exists to solve.

The trap: services that call each other

Here is how most people start coordinating multiple Lambdas: the orders service needs inventory reduced, a wallet charged, and an email sent. So it calls each in turn.

// inside the orders Lambda handler
await lambda.invoke({ FunctionName: "inventoryService", Payload: ... });
await lambda.invoke({ FunctionName: "paymentService",  Payload: ... });
await lambda.invoke({ FunctionName: "emailService",    Payload: ... });

This works on the demo, and it collapses in production. Each invoke is a synchronous hop with its own timeout1. Your order handler now fails if any downstream service is slow, down, or misconfigured. You have coupled the fate of ordering to the fate of email.

Key idea This coupling is the disease. Event-driven architecture is the treatment: a service publishes what happened, and any number of other services react to it — without the producer knowing or caring who they are.

Four failure modes of direct Lambda-to-Lambda calls

Failure modeWhat happens
Cascade timeoutsSlow downstream eats the caller's whole budget; the caller retries, adding load on an already-struggling service.
Fan-out by handTo notify three consumers you write three invoke calls and keep them in sync. A fourth consumer means editing the producer.
Tight couplingThe producer holds names/ARNs of consumers and must redeploy when a consumer appears, renames, or disappears.
Partial failurePayment succeeded but email failed. Who retries the email? Who knows payment already went through? Manual and error-prone.2

What an event changes

With an event bus, the orders service changes roles. It stops being a conductor and starts being a broadcaster:

  1. It publishes one event: Order Placed.
  2. Inventory, payment, and email each subscribe to events they care about.
  3. The producer has no idea any of them exist. Add a new consumer tomorrow and nothing in the producer changes.

This is the core trade of event-driven architecture: you give up a guaranteed synchronous result (knowing every step completed) in exchange for decoupling, independent scaling, and resilience — each consumer reacts at its own pace and can fail without blocking the producer.3

Two things are required for this to work, and they are the heart of this whole course:

  • A service that publishes events (the producer side).
  • A way to route each event to everyone who cares, and only to them (the rules side).

Amazon EventBridge is the AWS service that gives you both: a fully managed event bus that receives events and routes them to targets by matching rules. It is not a queue and not a topic — it is a router, and it is what the rest of this series builds on.4

Engineer's check If you ever wonder "should this communication become an event?", ask: does the caller need the result before it can continue, or does it just need to record that something happened? If the latter, an event is the right tool.
