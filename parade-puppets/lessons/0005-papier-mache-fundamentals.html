Papier-Mache Fundamentals

Lesson 5 Core technique ~2 hours (including drying time)

Papier-mache is the defining technique of parade puppet making. It turns cheap paper and paste into lightweight, rigid, paintable shells. Bread and Puppet Theater has built thousands of puppets with nothing more than cardboard soaked in cornstarch paste and dried in the sun. This lesson teaches you the technique before you apply it to your puppet head in Lesson 6.

The puppets are all made of papier-mache. They get sculpted in clay, which we dig out of the rivers here and mix with hay. Then over the clay mold, we put layers of cardboard that is soaked in water and corn starch, and left to harden in the sun. It's a process that takes a lot of work and no money whatsoever. — Peter Schumann, Bread and Puppet Theater (NPR, 2013)

Making the Paste: Cornstarch Goop

There are many papier-mache paste recipes. The community puppet standard is cornstarch goop, from the Cattywampus Puppet Council manual. It's cheap, non-toxic, dries hard, and smells like nothing.

The Recipe

  1. Mix 1 cup cornstarch with 1 cup cold water in a bowl. Stir until smooth — no lumps.
  2. In a pot, bring 4 cups of water to a boil.
  3. Slowly pour the cornstarch mixture into the boiling water, stirring constantly.
  4. Keep stirring over heat until the mixture thickens into a translucent, gluey consistency — like thick gravy. This takes 2–3 minutes.
  5. Remove from heat. Let it cool until you can comfortably put your hands in it.

This makes enough paste for one large puppet head. Scale up as needed — the ratio is always 1 part cornstarch : 5 parts water total.

Alternative: white glue paste. If you prefer, you can use white school glue thinned with water: 2 parts glue to 1 part water (Carousel Players guide). This is slightly stronger but costs more. Cornstarch goop is the traditional choice and works beautifully.

Preparing the Paper

The Cattywampus manual is clear: brown paper bags are the best paper for papier-mache. They're stronger and heavier than newspaper, producing a more rigid shell with fewer layers.

How to prepare strips:

  1. Tear, don't cut. Torn edges have exposed fibers that blend together smoothly when overlapped. Cut edges create visible, hard seams.
  2. Tear strips roughly 1 to 2 inches wide and 6 to 8 inches long. For detailed areas (around noses, eyes), tear smaller strips. For large flat areas, use wider strips.
  3. Tear a generous pile before you start pasting. You don't want to stop mid-layer with gluey hands to tear more paper.
The newspaper trick for armature shaping. Use newspaper for crumpling and shaping the rough form (the armature). Use brown paper bags for the papier-mache layers on top. They have different jobs: newspaper is cheap bulk, brown bags are structural skin.

Applying the Layers

This is the core skill. Here's the technique, step by step:

  1. Dip one strip into the goop so it's fully saturated.
  2. Squeeze off the excess by pulling the strip between two fingers. You want it wet but not dripping — like a wrung-out washcloth.
  3. Lay the strip onto your form, pressing it down flat and smooth. Work out air bubbles with your fingers. The strip should conform tightly to the surface beneath it.
  4. Overlap each strip with the previous one by about half its width. No gaps.
  5. Vary the direction. If the first strips go horizontally, lay the next ones vertically or diagonally. This cross-hatching dramatically increases strength — like plywood.

How many layers?

Armature type Recommended layers Why
Cardboard armature 2–4 layers The cardboard provides its own structure
Clay armature (to be removed) 4–6 layers The shell must be self-supporting after clay is removed
Tracking layers. The Cattywampus manual recommends alternating strips print side up and print side down between layers. This lets you see at a glance whether you've covered every spot in the current layer — if you can see print-up strips showing through, you've missed a spot.

Drying

Drying is where beginners make the most mistakes. Rushing kills puppets.

  • Apply 2 layers, then stop and dry. Don't try to apply all your layers in one session. Wet layers on wet layers slide around, trap moisture inside, and can grow mold.
  • Dry time: 12–24 hours in a well-ventilated area. In direct sun on a warm day, as fast as 4–6 hours.
  • Speed it up by pointing a fan at the piece. Do not use a hair dryer on high heat — uneven heating can warp the shape.
  • The piece is dry when it feels uniformly hard, makes a hollow sound when tapped, and has no dark wet spots.

After drying, apply your next 2 layers and dry again. Repeat until you've reached the target layer count.

The mold warning. In humid climates, papier-mache can develop mold if it stays damp too long. Work in a ventilated space, use a fan, and don't leave wet pieces in closed rooms overnight. If you see fuzzy spots, scrape them off and let the piece dry completely before adding more layers.

Practice Exercise

Before you make your puppet head, do a practice run on something small. This builds confidence and reveals problems while the stakes are low.

  1. Blow up a balloon to roughly the size of a softball.
  2. Mix a batch of cornstarch goop.
  3. Tear brown paper bag strips.
  4. Apply 3 layers of papier-mache over the balloon, leaving a small opening at the bottom (around the tied end).
  5. Let it dry fully (overnight).
  6. Pop the balloon and pull it out through the opening.

You now have a hollow papier-mache sphere. Squeeze it — feel how rigid it is. This is the material your puppet head will be made from. If it feels too fragile, you know to add more layers on the real build.

Check Your Understanding

Why should you tear papier-mache strips rather than cutting them with scissors?

Torn edges have small exposed paper fibers that interlock with adjacent strips and disappear into the surface. Cut edges are clean and hard, creating visible seams and ridges that are difficult to smooth out.

How many papier-mache layers does the Cattywampus manual recommend for a cardboard armature?

Two to four layers for a cardboard armature, because the cardboard itself provides structural rigidity. A clay armature (where the clay is removed afterward) needs 4–6 layers since the papier-mache shell must be fully self-supporting.

What is the purpose of alternating strip direction between layers?

Cross-hatching — laying strips horizontally in one layer and vertically in the next — dramatically increases the shell's rigidity. It works like plywood, where alternating grain directions create a material stronger than any single layer.
