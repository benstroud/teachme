Costuming & Assembly

Lesson 8 Hands-on build ~3–4 hours

You have a wearable frame (Lesson 4), a painted head (Lesson 6), and arms with control rods (Lesson 7). Now you bring it all together with fabric. The costume hides the skeleton, hides you, and gives the puppet its character. This is where a pile of PVC and cardboard becomes a giant walking figure.

Fabric Selection

From Lesson 1, you know that synthetic fabrics (nylon, polyester) are preferred over cotton — they're lighter, don't absorb water, and don't sag. But let's get more specific:

Fabric Best for Notes
Polyester bedsheets Robes, dresses, cloaks Cheapest option. Thrift stores. Look for king-size for maximum yardage.
Nylon / ripstop nylon Wings, flags, flowing accents Extremely light. Catches wind beautifully. Good for dynamic elements.
Fleece Fuzzy animal characters Heavier than synthetics, but the texture is worth it for the right character.
Felt Applique details, graphic shapes Doesn't fray when cut. Great for stars, patches, symbols, bold color accents.
Muslin / cotton Only if you plan to dye it Heavier, absorbs moisture. But takes Rit Dye beautifully for custom colors. (Cattywampus manual)
Nylon mesh Wind-prone areas, sails Air passes through — won't act as a sail in wind. Used by Avant Garb for their 12-foot parade puppets specifically for wind resistance.

How Much Fabric?

For an 8-foot backpack puppet with a full-length costume: roughly 12 yards of fabric (Peattie). That sounds like a lot, but the puppet's body is essentially a tent — fabric wraps all the way around, from shoulders to ground, and needs to be wide enough that you can move freely inside without the fabric pulling tight.

If you're using thrift-store bedsheets, you'll need 3–4 king-size flat sheets, sewn or stapled together.

Step 1: Draping the Body

The simplest approach — and the one used by most community puppet groups — is a poncho-style drape:

  1. Lay the fabric flat on the floor.
  2. Set the puppet frame upright on top of it (spine vertical, head mounted, arms extended). Center the fabric under the head.
  3. Drape the fabric up and over the head and crossbar, so it hangs down on all sides. The fabric should reach the ground (or your ankles) on all sides.
  4. Attach the fabric to the frame at key points:
    • Under the chin of the head — glue or staple the fabric to the spine for about 12 inches below the head (Carousel Players)
    • Along the underside of each arm — staple or glue to create "sleeves"
    • At the crossbar — tie or zip-tie to keep the fabric from sliding
  5. Trim the fabric into a rough garment shape. The Carousel Players guide suggests cutting a "t-shirt-like shape" — wider at the shoulders, tapering slightly at the sides, full-length at front and back.
The costume IS the body. As My Painted Dreams says: "You do not need to sculpt a perfect torso when a well-designed costume can suggest the shape from a distance." A loose robe flowing from broad shoulders implies a body. Let the fabric do the work.

Step 2: The Puppeteer's Visibility

You need to be able to see where you're going. Plan this before you close up the costume. Options:

  • Mesh window in the chest. Cut a rectangle in the front of the costume at your eye level. Cover it with dark mesh or sheer fabric from behind. You can see out; the audience can't see in.
  • Gap beneath the head. Leave a space between the bottom of the head and the top of the costume drape. Your view is the slit between head and body — like looking under a hat brim.
  • Vision slit in the mouth. If your puppet has an open mouth, you may be able to see through it, depending on how the head is angled.
  • Spotter walking alongside. For short parade routes, a companion can guide you verbally. This is a backup, not a primary solution.
Never rely on luck for visibility. My Painted Dreams puts it perfectly: "Never rely on luck when your character has a wingspan wider than a shopping cart." Test your sightlines before the costume is finished. Walk through a doorway. Step over an obstacle. If you can't see the ground 6 feet ahead, fix it now.

Step 3: Access Panels

You need to get in and out of the puppet. You also need to reach the frame for repairs, adjust straps, and access the control rods. Build in hidden openings:

  • Velcro panels — sew or glue Velcro strips along a seam in the back. You can open the costume from behind to step in and strap on the frame, then close it up.
  • Overlapping fabric — instead of closing a seam, let two panels overlap. You can reach through the overlap without any fasteners.
  • Ties or snaps — at the bottom hem, so you can lift the costume to step out.

The My Painted Dreams guide recommends: "Leave hidden openings for repairs. Velcro panels, ties, snaps, or overlapping fabric sections allow you to reach the frame, control rods, and backpack straps without dismantling the entire puppet."

Step 4: Final Assembly

Here's the complete assembly sequence:

  1. Put on the backpack frame (without the head). Adjust straps. Comfortable?
  2. Have a helper drape the costume over the spine and crossbar.
  3. Slide the head onto the top of the spine. The mounting tube seats over the pipe. The head should rest securely but be removable.
  4. Connect the control rods. Reach through the costume to grip the rod handles.
  5. Close the access panel behind you (or have your helper do it).
  6. Check visibility. Can you see the ground ahead?
  7. Walk.
Backpack puppets are built on a pole which is attached to a backpack and worn like a regular backpack. The advantage is that the movement is very natural; the puppet simply does everything you do. The disadvantage is that it takes help to put it on. — Sara Peattie, 68 Ways to Make Really Big Puppets

Yes — you will generally need a helper to put the puppet on and take it off. This is normal. Every backpack puppeteer needs a "dresser."

Step 5: Detailing the Costume

Once the basic drape works, add character:

  • Belt or sash — gathers the fabric at the "waist," giving the costume shape
  • Collar or neckline — frames the head, hides the mounting tube
  • Applique details — felt shapes (stars, moons, flowers, symbols) glued or sewn to the fabric
  • Ribbon or trim — along hems and sleeves for finished edges
  • Color accents — strips of contrasting fabric sewn or stapled on

Keep details lightweight and secure. Anything dangling loosely will catch on things during a parade. Anything heavy will drag the fabric down.

Check Your Understanding

Why does the Avant Garb company use nylon mesh for their parade puppets?

A large puppet in wind is essentially a sail. Solid fabric catches wind and can pull the puppeteer off balance or even knock them over. Nylon mesh lets air pass through while still providing visual coverage. This is especially important for tall puppets with large surface areas.

How much fabric does a standard 8-foot backpack puppet require for a full costume?

Sara Peattie's 68 Ways to Make Really Big Puppets notes that a backpack puppet with an 8-foot upright takes about 12 yards of cloth for the costume and hair. That's roughly 3–4 king-size flat sheets if sourcing from a thrift store.
