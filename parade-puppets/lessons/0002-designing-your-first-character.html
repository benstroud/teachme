Designing Your First Character

Lesson 2 No drawing skill required ~12 minutes

You know the five parts of a backpack puppet. Now you need to decide what to build. This lesson walks you through choosing a character that's achievable for a first build, and sketching it out just enough to start planning materials.

The First-Puppet Trap

Most first-time puppet builders imagine something ambitious: a dragon with moving wings, a skeleton with jointed limbs, a monarch with an elaborate crown. These are wonderful second or third puppets. As a first puppet, they're a recipe for an unfinished project in your garage.

The goal of your first puppet is to finish it. Not to make it perfect. Not to make it complex. To get a complete, wearable puppet that you can walk around in. Everything you learn doing that will make your second puppet dramatically better.

The My Painted Dreams guide suggests a "friendly garden giant" as a first project: an oversized human figure in a flowing robe, with a big expressive face and simple hands. That's an excellent template because it keeps every part simple.

What Makes a Character Beginner-Friendly?

Rate your character idea against these five constraints:

Constraint Beginner-friendly Avoid for now
Head shape Round or oval (human, sun, moon, owl) Complex geometry (dragon snout, antlers)
Body shape Hidden by a robe, dress, or cloak Exposed body requiring sculpted torso
Limbs Two simple arms, no legs visible Wings, tails, extra limbs, visible legs
Movement Arms wave via control rods (or no arm movement) Moving jaw, blinking eyes, articulated fingers
Operators One person (you) Multiple operators coordinating

If your idea scores "beginner-friendly" in at least 4 of 5 categories, it's a good first puppet.

Good First Characters

Here are character archetypes that work well as first builds, drawn from the Puppeteers' Cooperative patterns and community parade traditions:

  • The Giant — a large, friendly humanoid figure. Big round head, flowing robe, simple hands. This is the classic. Think a wise old wizard, a gentle grandmother, a sun king.
  • The Ghost/Spirit — a head on a pole with long, flowing white fabric. Extremely simple construction. Eerily beautiful in motion. The fabric does all the work.
  • The Flower — a large flower head (petals made from fabric or foam) on a green-draped body. The head is flat-ish (a circle with petals), which simplifies construction.
  • The Skeleton — sounds complex, but a simple version is just a white head on black fabric, with white fabric hands. The "bones" are implied by color contrast. Peattie's book includes a backpack skeleton design.
  • The Bird — a round head with a beak (a cone on a sphere), with fabric "wings" that drape like a cloak. The flapping-wing version from the Puppeteers' Cooperative uses a simple crosspiece mechanism.

Sketching Your Puppet

You don't need to be able to draw. You need three rough sketches:

  1. Front view — the face and body shape as a parade-goer would see it
  2. Side view — how far the puppet extends from your back, where the head sits relative to your body
  3. The skeleton — just the frame, spine, crossbar, and where the head mounts. No costume, no beauty. Stick figures are fine.

On each sketch, mark these measurements:

  • Total height — from ground to top of head. For a first puppet, aim for 7 to 8 feet. Taller requires a longer spine and more costume fabric, and gets harder to balance.
  • Head size — diameter or width. A 2-foot-wide head is big enough to read from a distance without being unwieldy.
  • Arm span — fingertip to fingertip. Keep the arms shorter than your own reach, or you won't be able to work the control rods.
Build a miniature first. Before committing to full-size materials, build a quick maquette (a small model) from cardboard, tape, and a stick. Even a 12-inch version reveals problems: "the head is too wide for the body," "the arms look too short," "this shape is going to be really hard to papier-mache." Five minutes with cardboard can save five hours with lumber. (My Painted Dreams)

Planning Questions

Before you finalize your character, answer these practical questions from the Cattywampus manual:

  1. Where will you build it? You need a workspace where wet papier-mache can dry undisturbed for a day or two. A garage, covered patio, or spare room works. A small apartment kitchen does not.
  2. Where will you store it? A finished 8-foot puppet won't fit in a closet. Plan for vertical storage — a garage corner, a shed, hanging from rafters.
  3. How will you transport it? Can the head detach from the spine? Will it fit in (or on top of) your car? Detachable heads are strongly recommended — the Puppeteers' Cooperative "box" joint method from Lesson 1 is designed for this.
  4. Can you see out of it? You need a vision slit or mesh panel somewhere. Plan for it now, not after the costume is sewn.

Check Your Understanding

What is the most important goal for your first puppet?

Finishing is the goal. A complete simple puppet teaches you far more than an abandoned ambitious one. Your second puppet will benefit from everything you learn by actually completing the first.

Why is a "flowing robe" body style recommended for beginners?

A loose robe, cloak, or dress suggests a body shape without requiring you to actually sculpt one. The fabric does the visual work, hiding the bare frame and spine underneath. This means less construction and a more forgiving build.

What is a "maquette" in puppet design?

A maquette is a quick miniature prototype — typically 12 inches or so — made from scrap cardboard and tape. It lets you see proportions, spot structural problems, and test ideas before committing to full-size materials.

Your Task

Before the next lesson:

  1. Choose a character concept that scores "beginner-friendly" in at least 4 of 5 categories from the table above.
  2. Make three rough sketches (front, side, skeleton). Stick figures are fine.
  3. Write down your answers to the four planning questions.
  4. Optional: build a quick cardboard maquette.
