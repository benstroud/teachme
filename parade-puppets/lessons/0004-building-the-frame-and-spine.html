Building the Frame & Spine

Lesson 4 Hands-on build ~1 hour active work

This is your first real build session. By the end, you'll have a wearable frame with a vertical spine that you can strap on and walk around in. No head, no costume — just the skeleton. This is the foundation everything else attaches to.

You'll need: an external-frame backpack, a 3/4" PVC pipe (8 ft), 2–3 hose clamps, a screwdriver, duct tape, a measuring tape, and a marker. See the full list in Lesson 3.

Step 1: Prepare the Backpack

If your thrift-store backpack still has its bag attached, remove it. You only need the frame and harness — the rigid structure with its shoulder straps, hip belt, and any horizontal tension straps.

  1. Strip off the bag. Most are attached with pins, clips, or simple stitching. Keep the hip belt and shoulder straps.
  2. Put the empty frame on. Adjust it to fit you. The hip belt should sit on your hip bones (not your waist). The shoulder straps should be snug but not digging in. Walk around for a minute.
  3. Have someone grab the frame and try to shift it side-to-side and up-and-down while you're wearing it. It should barely move. If it rocks, tighten the straps further. (Sherry Clark's guide)
Fit is everything. A poorly fitted frame means an exhausting, unstable puppet. Spend real time here. If the hip belt is too loose or the shoulder straps too long, the puppet will lean and sway uncontrollably. The weight should rest on your hips, with your shoulders only balancing.

Step 2: Cut the Spine

Your spine is a single piece of 3/4" PVC pipe. To determine the length:

  1. Decide on your puppet's total height. For a first puppet, 7–8 feet is recommended.
  2. Measure from the top of the backpack frame (where you'll attach the spine) to the ground. This is your "already have" height.
  3. Subtract that from your target puppet height. The remainder is your spine length. For most people, this is roughly 3 to 4 feet of pipe rising above the frame.
Cut long, trim later. As Sherry Clark's guide puts it: "Measure twice, cut once." But also: always start longer than you think you need. You can always shorten pipe — you can't add length back. Mark your cut line with a permanent marker, double-check, then cut with a PVC cutter or hacksaw.

After cutting, clean up the cut end. Remove any burrs with sandpaper or a utility knife so it's smooth.

Step 3: Attach the Spine to the Frame

The spine needs to be vertical and rigid — no wobbling, no leaning. There are two common methods:

Method A: Hose Clamps (Recommended for Beginners)

  1. Hold the PVC pipe against the vertical bars of the backpack frame. Position it so the pipe sits against the frame's center, rising straight up from behind your head.
  2. Wrap 2–3 hose clamps around both the pipe and the frame bar, spacing them 8–12 inches apart.
  3. Tighten each hose clamp firmly with a screwdriver. The pipe should not rotate or slide.
  4. Reinforce with duct tape wrapped around both pipe and frame between the clamps. Belt and suspenders.

Method B: Bolts Through Frame

If your frame has flat metal bars, you can drill holes through both the frame bar and the PVC pipe, then bolt them together with machine bolts, washers, and wing nuts. This is more permanent and rigid than hose clamps, but requires a drill and is harder to undo if you need to adjust.

The lumbar pillow trick. Sherry Clark recommends stuffing a small pillow or rolled towel between the spine and your lower back. This does two things: adds comfort padding, and tilts the spine slightly backward, preventing the puppet from leaning forward over your head. A forward-leaning puppet is exhausting and makes you hunch.

Step 4: The Balance Test

Put the frame on with the spine attached. Walk around your workspace for at least five minutes. Pay attention to:

  • Does the spine tilt forward? If so, add the lumbar pillow or reposition the attachment point higher on the frame.
  • Does it wobble side to side? Tighten the clamps, add more attachment points, or add a stabilizing brace.
  • Can you turn your head freely? The spine must clear your head with room to spare. If it's too close, you'll constantly bump it. Adjust the attachment position if needed.
  • Can you see the ground? You need to be able to look down enough to walk safely. Check this now.
  • Is the weight on your hips? If your shoulders are bearing the load, adjust the hip belt.

Step 5: Mark Attachment Points

While the frame is working well, use a marker to note where you'll attach things later:

  • Shoulder crossbar position — about 12–18 inches above the frame top. Mark the spine here. This is where the arms will mount in Lesson 7.
  • Head mount position — the very top of the spine. If you're using the "box" joint from Lesson 1, the head will slide over this end.
  • Costume attachment zone — the area where fabric will be tied or stapled. Just below the head, along the crossbar, and down the spine.

Check Your Understanding

Why should you stuff a lumbar pillow between the spine and your lower back?

A lumbar pillow tilts the spine slightly backward. Without it, the weight of the head tends to pull the puppet forward, making you hunch and straining your back. The pillow also adds padding for comfort.

When attaching the spine to the frame with hose clamps, how many should you use?

Two to three hose clamps, spaced 8–12 inches apart along the frame, provide secure attachment with enough redundancy. One clamp is a single point of failure; too many adds weight and complexity without benefit. Reinforce between clamps with duct tape.

Your Task

Build this. The entire frame-and-spine assembly should take under an hour. When you're done, walk around with it for 10 minutes. If it's comfortable and stable, you've nailed the hardest engineering decision. Everything that follows is decoration.
