Arms, Hands & Control Rods

Lesson 7 Hands-on build ~2 hours

Arms give your puppet the ability to gesture, wave, and interact with the crowd. They're the main way a backpack puppet "performs" beyond simply walking. In this lesson you'll build a shoulder crossbar, two arms with hands, and the control rods that let you operate them from inside the costume.

The Decision: Moving Arms or Passive Arms?

Before building, decide which approach fits your first puppet:

Approach How it works Difficulty
Passive arms Fabric sleeves stuffed lightly, hanging from the crossbar. They sway as you walk. No control rods needed. Very easy
Single moving arm One arm has a control rod, one is passive. You operate the moving arm with your dominant hand. Easy
Two moving arms Both arms have control rods. You operate one in each hand. More expressive, but your hands are occupied. Moderate

For a first puppet, a single moving arm is the sweet spot. It gives the puppet personality (it can wave, point, gesture) while leaving your other hand free to manage the costume, carry things, or steady the frame.

Step 1: Build the Shoulder Crossbar

The crossbar is a horizontal bar attached to the spine at "shoulder height" on the puppet. The arms hang from its ends.

  1. Take a piece of 1/2" PVC pipe, about 2–3 feet long. This is the puppet's "shoulder width." Wider shoulders look more imposing but catch more wind.
  2. Attach it to the spine using a PVC T-connector. The spine passes through the vertical opening of the T; the crossbar goes through the horizontal opening. If the sizes don't match perfectly (3/4" spine into a 1/2" T), use duct tape to shim the joint tight, or use a reducing T-connector.
  3. Position the crossbar about 12–18 inches above the top of the backpack frame — this is the mark you made in Lesson 4. The crossbar should be well above your head but below the puppet's chin.
Don't glue yet. Friction-fit the T-connector for now. You'll want to test the puppet's proportions and balance before making anything permanent. Sherry Clark's guide is emphatic: "Gluing is permanent. Therefore it is essential to test puppet dimensions, clearances, visibility, etc. before gluing up the joints."

Step 2: Build the Arm Bones

Arms can be made from:

  • 1/2" PVC pipe — rigid, easy to cut, connects to the crossbar
  • Wooden dowels — lighter, slightly more natural bounce
  • Cardboard tubes — the lightest option, but least durable

For each arm:

  1. Cut a piece of arm material 18–24 inches long. This is shorter than a real arm — remember, the costume fabric will extend the visual length, and too-long arms become unwieldy. (My Painted Dreams)
  2. Attach the arm to the end of the crossbar. For PVC-to-PVC, use an elbow connector so the arm angles slightly downward. For dowels, use rope, fabric hinges, or zip ties to create a flexible joint. A flexible joint at the shoulder is better than a rigid one — it lets the arm swing naturally as you walk.

Step 3: Make the Hands

Puppet hands should be simple, oversized, and lightweight. The My Painted Dreams guide is direct: "Hands do not need individual moving fingers. Foam mittens, fabric gloves stuffed with batting, cardboard claws, or sculpted foam paws are easier to control."

Method A: Cardboard Hands (Simplest)

  1. Draw a mitten-shaped hand on cardboard — about 10–12 inches long, with a separate thumb. Don't try to make individual fingers on a first puppet.
  2. Cut two identical shapes per hand.
  3. For a 3D hand: curve the top layer, staple the edges to the bottom layer, and stuff with crumpled newspaper in between. (Carousel Players guide)
  4. Papier-mache over the hand (2 layers), dry, prime, and paint. The same process as the head, but faster because it's smaller.

Method B: Foam Hands (Faster)

  1. Cut hand shapes from EVA foam sheets (3/8" to 1" thick). These are available at craft stores.
  2. Paint with paint made for plastics, or cover with fabric.
  3. Hot-glue to the end of the arm bone.

The Cattywampus manual includes a cardboard hand template (both single and double hand) that you can scale up for your puppet.

Step 4: Attach Control Rods

The control rod is what makes an arm "alive." It's a thin stick connecting the puppet's wrist to the puppeteer's hand inside the costume.

  1. Take a 3/8" wooden dowel, about 3 feet long. This needs to reach from the puppet's hand down to your hand inside the costume.
  2. Attach the top end to the puppet's wrist or forearm (not the hand — the wrist gives better leverage). Use hot glue and reinforce with a wrap of duct tape or a zip tie.
  3. The bottom end is your handle. Leave it bare, or add a short crosspiece of dowel for a T-grip, which is easier to hold during a long parade.

Testing the Control Rod

Hold the control rod at the bottom handle. The puppet's arm should respond to your movements:

  • Push up — the puppet's arm rises (waving)
  • Pull down — the arm drops
  • Rotate your wrist — the hand should swivel slightly

If the arm flops or doesn't respond, the rod attachment at the wrist may be too loose. Reinforce it. If the arm is too heavy to raise easily, the arm bone or hand is too heavy — lighten them.

The "Look, Think, Move" rule. When performing, don't just wave the arm randomly. My Painted Dreams suggests: before the puppet moves, let it look toward something. Pause. Then move. That tiny delay creates the impression of thought — it's the difference between a puppet and a stick with a hand on it.

Check Your Understanding

Why should you use a flexible joint (rope, fabric hinge) at the shoulder rather than a rigid connection?

A flexible shoulder joint lets the arm bounce and sway as the puppeteer walks, creating natural-looking movement without any effort. A rigid joint makes the arm stick out stiffly, looking mechanical and dead.

Where should the control rod attach to the puppet's arm?

The wrist gives the best balance of leverage and control. Attaching at the fingertips gives too little leverage (hard to lift the arm). Attaching at the shoulder gives no range of motion. The wrist lets you raise, lower, and rotate the arm with natural-feeling movements.
