Testing, Repair & Parade Day

Lesson 9 Final lesson ~10 minutes reading + rehearsal time

Your puppet is assembled. Before you walk it in public, you need to test it, know how to fix it, and prepare for the realities of performing outdoors. This final lesson covers the gap between "it works in my garage" and "it works in a parade."

The Dress Rehearsal

Put the fully assembled puppet on — head, costume, control rods, everything — and walk around for at least ten minutes. The My Painted Dreams guide recommends testing the following:

  1. Walk slowly. Parade pace is slower than normal walking. Get used to the weight and sway.
  2. Turn around. Can you turn 180 degrees without the head swinging dangerously? Without the costume wrapping around you?
  3. Go through a doorway. Will your puppet fit through the staging area entrance? Know your width and height.
  4. Look down. Can you see the ground at least 6 feet ahead? Can you spot a curb, a child, a pothole?
  5. Sit down (if your design allows). Can you rest during breaks?
  6. Ask a helper to watch for dangerous swinging parts, loose fabric that could trip you, and anything that looks like it might fall off.
Check height against overhead hazards. Sherry Clark's guide warns: "If your puppet is to be in parades or used outdoors in public places you must keep the overall height shorter than low-hanging wires and branches. For safety, this should be less than 12 feet." Know your puppet's exact height and scout the parade route for low-hanging hazards.

Common Problems and Fixes

Problem Cause Fix
Head wobbles or spins Mounting tube too loose on spine Wrap masking tape around the spine to increase diameter. Or add a foam shim inside the tube for friction.
Puppet leans forward Weight too far from your back; no lumbar pillow Add the lumbar pillow from Lesson 4. Reposition the spine attachment higher on the frame. Lighten the head.
Shoulders ache after 5 minutes Weight on shoulders instead of hips Tighten the hip belt. Loosen the shoulder straps slightly. The hip belt carries the load; shoulders just balance.
Can't see Vision panel too small or in wrong position Enlarge the mesh window. Reposition it to match your actual eye level when wearing the frame.
Arms don't respond to control rods Loose attachment at wrist; rod too flexible Reinforce the wrist joint with more hot glue and duct tape. Use a stiffer dowel if the rod bends.
Costume drags or trips you Too long at the bottom Trim the hem. It should clear the ground by 2–3 inches. Better too short than tangled in your feet.
Wind catches the puppet like a sail Large solid fabric surface area Cut strategic slits in the back of the costume. Use nylon mesh for large panels. Remove excess fabric bulk.

Weatherproofing

Parade day weather is unpredictable. Prepare for the worst:

  • Rain: Papier-mache and water are enemies. Seal the head with 2–3 coats of polyurethane spray or clear acrylic sealer before the event. This creates a water-resistant shell. For the costume, synthetic fabrics naturally resist water better than cotton.
  • Sun: Acrylic paint can fade in prolonged UV. A clear UV-resistant spray sealer helps. More importantly, the puppeteer inside gets hot — wear light clothing, hydrate, and take breaks.
  • Wind: The biggest physical threat. A gust can turn your puppet into a sail and pull you off balance. The Avant Garb solution: use nylon mesh. You can also add strategic slits or vents in the back of the costume. In strong winds, have a spotter walk behind you to help stabilize.
Know your exit. Before the parade, identify where along the route you can safely step out if something breaks, if you overheat, or if the wind becomes dangerous. Never feel obligated to continue if something is wrong. A person falling over in a puppet is a safety hazard for everyone around them — especially children.

Storage and Transport

Your puppet needs to survive between events:

  • Detach the head for transport. This is why the box joint from Lesson 1 is designed to be removable.
  • Store vertically if possible — hanging from a rafter, leaning in a garage corner, or standing in a shed. Horizontal storage can crush the head or warp the costume.
  • Keep dry. Papier-mache absorbs moisture from the air. Store in a dry space, not an unfinished basement.
  • Transport in a vehicle with the head wrapped in a sheet or plastic bag. The spine and frame can often ride in the back seat or trunk if the head is detached. Measure your car's interior before parade day — you don't want to discover a fit problem at the last minute.

Performance Tips

Once the engineering is solid, the puppet's impact comes from how you move it:

  • Walk slowly. Parade pace is about half your normal walking speed. Slow movement reads as deliberate and grand. Fast movement looks panicked.
  • Use the "Look, Think, Move" rule from Lesson 7. Before the puppet moves, let it look at something. Pause. Then move. This creates the illusion of a thinking being.
  • Engage the crowd. Wave at children. Point at things. Turn the puppet's head to "look" at people along the route. The puppet should feel present, not like it's just passing by.
  • Move the whole body. Don't just wave the control rod. Lean, sway, turn. The backpack puppet transmits your body language — that's its greatest advantage.
  • Rest the arm. If you have one moving arm, you don't need to wave it constantly. Let it rest at the puppet's side, then bring it up for specific moments — a wave, a greeting, a dramatic point. Restraint makes each gesture more meaningful.

As you create that movement and sound day to day, learning the subtle difference between demure gliding and sluglike trailing... you get deeply familiar with your puppets. — Wit's End Puppets, on apprenticing at Bread and Puppet Theater

Your Parade Checklist

The day of the event, bring:

  • The puppet (head detached, costume folded)
  • Duct tape — the universal field repair
  • Zip ties — backup fastening
  • A screwdriver — for hose clamp adjustments
  • Masking tape — for quick shims and fixes
  • Water and a snack — for the human inside
  • Sunscreen — you'll be outside for hours
  • A helper/dresser — someone to help you put it on and take it off
  • A phone number for the event organizer — in case you need to communicate

Check Your Understanding

What is the most important thing to check during your dress rehearsal?

Visibility is a safety issue, not just a comfort issue. If you can't see the ground 6 feet ahead, you can trip on curbs, step on children, or walk into obstacles. Everything else is secondary to being able to see where you're going.

How should you protect a papier-mache puppet head from rain?

Two to three coats of clear polyurethane spray or acrylic sealer create a water-resistant barrier over the papier-mache. This should be done well before parade day. Wrapping in plastic would hide the face; extra layers add weight; oil-based paint alone isn't waterproof enough.

Why should the puppet walk at about half normal walking speed in a parade?

A giant figure moving slowly looks majestic and intentional. The same figure moving quickly looks frantic and out of control. Slow, deliberate movement is how you sell the illusion that this is a giant being walking among humans, not a person in a costume rushing to get somewhere.

What's Next

You've completed the core curriculum. You now have the knowledge to design, build, and perform a backpack parade puppet from scratch. Here's where to go from here:

  • Build your first puppet. If you haven't already started during the lessons, now is the time. Go back to Lesson 2, choose your character, and work through the build sequence.
  • Find a parade. Search for local community parades, arts festivals, Halloween events, or solstice celebrations. Many welcome walk-in performers with puppets. Some have organized puppet contingents.
  • Join a community. The Puppeteers' Cooperative in Boston operates a Puppet Library. Puppeteers of America connects practitioners nationally. The Cattywampus Puppet Council in Knoxville runs community builds. Bread and Puppet Theater in Vermont hosts summer apprenticeships and community circuses.
  • Build a second puppet. Now that you've finished one, you know what worked and what didn't. Your second puppet will be dramatically better. Try a more complex character — maybe one with a moving jaw, or a two-person dragon, or a bird with flapping wings from the Puppeteers' Cooperative patterns.
