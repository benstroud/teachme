Anatomy of a Backpack Puppet

Lesson 1 No craft experience required ~10 minutes

Before you build anything, you need to understand what you're building. This lesson teaches you the five structural parts of a backpack parade puppet and how they work together. By the end, you'll be able to look at any backpack puppet and name its components.

What Is a Backpack Puppet?

A backpack puppet is a large figure — usually 7 to 12 feet tall — worn on a frame strapped to the puppeteer's back, like a hiking pack. The puppet's body towers above the puppeteer, whose own body is typically hidden inside the costume.

Backpack puppets are built on a pole which is attached to a backpack and worn like a regular backpack. The advantage is that the movement is very natural; the puppet simply does everything you do. — Sara Peattie, 68 Ways to Make Really Big Puppets

This is why backpack puppets are ideal for parades: the puppet mirrors the puppeteer's walking, turning, and swaying. No strings, no complex rigging — just a person walking down the street, wearing a giant.

The Five Parts

Every backpack puppet, no matter how elaborate, breaks down into the same five structural components:

Let's walk through each one.

1. The Frame

The frame is the wearable base — what transfers the puppet's weight to your body. The most common approach: buy a used external-frame backpack from a thrift store. These packs already have padded shoulder straps and hip belts designed to carry heavy loads comfortably.

Why "external frame"? External-frame backpacks (like old Kelty or ALICE packs) have a rigid metal or plastic frame separate from the bag. This frame gives you something solid to bolt or strap your puppet's spine to. Internal-frame packs (most modern hiking packs) don't work as well because there's no exposed rigid structure.

The frame is arguably the most important structural decision. If the frame doesn't fit comfortably, the puppet becomes exhausting to wear — and in a parade, you may be walking for an hour or more. The weight should rest primarily on your hips, not your shoulders. Sherry Clark's puppet construction guide recommends stuffing a small lumbar pillow between the frame and your back to tilt the spine slightly backward, preventing the puppet from leaning forward.

2. The Spine

The spine is a single vertical pole that rises from the frame up to where the head sits. It's the puppet's backbone. Common materials:

Material Pros Cons
Bamboo pole Free, strong, lightweight, traditional Can split; harder to attach fittings
PVC pipe (3/4" to 1") Cheap, easy to cut, connectors available Can flex under load; not as elegant
Wooden dowel or 1"x2" lumber Rigid, easy to screw into Heavier than bamboo or PVC

For an 8-foot-tall puppet, the spine typically rises about 2 to 3 feet above the puppeteer's head. The spine attaches to the frame with hose clamps, bolts, heavy-duty zip ties, or duct tape — whatever holds it rigid and vertical.

3. The Head

The head is usually the most visually striking part. It sits on top of the spine and is made to be as light as possible — because every ounce up top is amplified by the leverage of the spine.

Common construction methods:

  • Papier-mache over a cardboard armature — the classic approach. Build a rough shape from crumpled newspaper and cardboard, then cover with layers of paper strips soaked in cornstarch paste or white glue. The Appalachian Puppet Pageant manual recommends brown paper bags over newspaper for strength, and 2 to 4 layers on a cardboard armature.
  • Foam (EVA, upholstery, or pool noodles) — lightweight and fast. Good for simpler shapes.
  • Cardboard box — the simplest option. A box on a pole can become a robot, a house, an animal head.
The cardinal rule of puppet heads: Exaggerate everything. Large eyes, bold colors, dramatic features. From 20 feet away in a crowd, subtle details disappear. Think billboard, not portrait.

The head attaches to the spine using a "box" joint — a slightly oversized box or tube that slides over the pole, loose enough to let the head sway naturally but tight enough that it can't spin freely. Peattie's guide details several attachment methods, including square lashing with paracord for bamboo poles.

4. The Arms (and Hands)

Arms extend out from a crossbar attached to the spine, typically at "shoulder height" on the puppet. They're made from lightweight materials — PVC, dowels, foam tubes — and end in oversized hands made from cardboard, foam, or papier-mache.

The key feature: control rods. Thin sticks or dowels run from the puppet's wrists down to the puppeteer's hands inside the costume. When you raise your hand, the puppet raises its hand. This is the primary way a backpack puppet "performs."

Simple puppets may have no arm movement at all — just fabric sleeves that sway as you walk. That's a perfectly valid choice for a first puppet.

5. The Costume

The costume is the fabric that covers the skeleton, hides the puppeteer, and gives the puppet its character. It drapes from the head and shoulders down to the ground (or the puppeteer's ankles).

Material choice matters more than you'd think:

  • Nylon, polyester, and synthetic blends are preferred — they're lighter, don't absorb water, and don't sag. (Cattywampus manual)
  • Cotton and muslin work if you're dyeing the fabric, but they're heavier and retain moisture.
  • Old bedsheets and thrift-store curtains are the budget-friendly go-to.

An 8-foot puppet with a full costume needs roughly 12 yards of fabric (Peattie). The costume should have hidden openings (velcro, ties) so the puppeteer can get in and out, and so you can reach the frame for repairs.

The costume is your secret weapon. A well-designed robe, cloak, or dress can hide an incredibly simple skeleton underneath. You don't need to sculpt a perfect body when loose fabric suggests the shape from a distance. (My Painted Dreams)

How It All Connects

The build sequence, in the simplest terms:

  1. Start with the frame. Get a backpack that fits you. Everything else depends on this.
  2. Attach the spine. A vertical pole, secured to the frame.
  3. Add the head. Built separately, then mounted on top of the spine.
  4. Add shoulders and arms. A crossbar on the spine, with arm sticks and optional control rods.
  5. Drape the costume. Fabric from head to ground, hiding the structure and the puppeteer.

That's it. Every elaborate parade puppet you've ever seen is a variation on this sequence.

Check Your Understanding

Where should the weight of a backpack puppet primarily rest on the puppeteer's body?

The hips. Just like a good hiking pack, the hip belt carries the load. Shoulder straps balance the puppet, but the hips do the heavy lifting. This is why external-frame packs with hip belts are the preferred base.

Why should puppet heads use exaggerated features rather than realistic details?

Parade puppets are seen from 20+ feet away in a crowd. Subtle realistic details simply vanish at that distance. Bold eyes, oversized noses, and dramatic colors read clearly even from far away — like a billboard, not a portrait.

What is a "control rod" on a backpack puppet?

A control rod is a thin stick or dowel connecting a puppet's wrist to the puppeteer's hand. When you move your hand, the puppet's hand moves. It's the primary way a single-operator backpack puppet "acts."

Why are synthetic fabrics (nylon, polyester) preferred over cotton for puppet costumes?

Cotton is heavier, absorbs rain and sweat, and sags when wet. Synthetics stay light and dry. In a parade — potentially in summer heat or unexpected rain — that difference matters enormously over an hour of walking.

Recommended Reading

Your primary source for this topic is the free Giant Puppet Making 101 Building Manual from the Appalachian Puppet Pageant / Cattywampus Puppet Council. It's a well-illustrated PDF that covers planning, papier-mache, armatures, backpack frames, and materials — exactly what you'll need for the lessons ahead. Skim it now; we'll dig into specific sections in future lessons.

If you want to go deeper, the book 68 Ways to Make Really Big Puppets by Sara Peattie is the foundational reference. It's $8 from the Bread & Puppet Press. A partial PDF is freely available.
