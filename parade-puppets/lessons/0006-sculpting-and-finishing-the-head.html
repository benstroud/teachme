Sculpting & Finishing the Head

Lesson 6 Hands-on build ~3–4 hours (spread over 2–3 days for drying)

The head is the soul of your puppet. It's what the audience looks at, remembers, and connects with. In this lesson you'll build a puppet head from cardboard and newspaper, cover it in papier-mache, and paint it. You learned the papier-mache technique in Lesson 5 — now you'll apply it for real.

Choosing Your Armature Method

The Cattywampus manual describes three armature methods. For a first puppet head, we'll use the cardboard armature — it's the simplest and doesn't require clay or mold-making.

Method Best for Difficulty
Cardboard armature Simple, bold shapes (round heads, geometric faces) Beginner
Clay over bucket armature Detailed, fluid 360-degree heads Intermediate
Clay on paper armature Flat masks and 180-degree faces Intermediate

Step 1: Build the Cardboard Armature

You're building a rough 3D shape that the papier-mache will cover. It doesn't need to be pretty — it needs to be the right size and approximate shape.

  1. Start with the basic form. For a round human head, cut an oval from a large piece of single-wall cardboard, roughly 2 feet tall by 1.5 feet wide. (Carousel Players guide)
  2. Create dimension with darts. Draw a second oval line 3 inches outside the first. Cut out along the outer line. Then cut triangular "darts" from the outer edge inward to the inner line, evenly spaced around the perimeter. Fold the 3-inch flaps inward along the inner line and staple where they overlap. This curves the flat cardboard into a bowl shape — instant 3D.
  3. Build up features. Crumple newspaper into balls and wads to form the nose, brow ridges, cheekbones, and chin. Tape them in place with masking tape. Use strips of cardboard for harder structural details. The Carousel Players guide notes that "thin strips of cardboard add detailing for parts that need to be more solid."
  4. Create the mounting tube. At the bottom of the head (the chin/neck area), you need a tube or open box that will slide over the top of your spine. Make a cardboard cylinder or box slightly wider than your spine pipe. Tape and staple it to the inside bottom of the head. This is your "box joint" — the head will rest on the spine by gravity, held in place by this sleeve.
Remember: exaggerate everything. From Lesson 1: a parade audience sees your puppet from 20+ feet away. Make the nose twice as big as you think it should be. Make the eyes enormous. Subtlety is invisible at parade distance.

Step 2: Papier-Mache the Head

Using the technique from Lesson 5:

  1. Mix a batch of cornstarch goop.
  2. Tear brown paper bag strips (1–2 inches wide).
  3. Apply 2 layers over the entire head, covering all the cardboard and newspaper features. Vary strip direction between layers. Press strips into all the contours of the face.
  4. Stop and dry — 12–24 hours, or faster with a fan.
  5. Apply 2 more layers. Dry again.

After drying, the head should feel hard and drum-like when you tap it. If any area feels soft or flexible, add another layer there and dry again.

Lightening the head. Once the papier-mache is fully dry, reach in from the back and pull out as much newspaper stuffing as you can. The papier-mache shell is now self-supporting; the newspaper was just scaffolding. Removing it makes the head significantly lighter. (Carousel Players guide)

Step 3: Prime and Paint

Painting happens after the head is completely dry. Any residual moisture under paint will cause bubbling and peeling.

  1. Prime with white. Apply a coat of gesso or white acrylic paint over the entire surface. This gives you a clean, neutral base and seals the papier-mache. Let it dry.
  2. Base colors. Paint the main color of the face (skin tone, green for a monster, blue for a spirit — whatever your character needs). Use acrylic paint or latex house paint. The Cattywampus manual notes that used house paint from recycling centers is often free.
  3. Features and shading. Paint eyes, mouth, eyebrows, and any detail with high contrast. The Carousel Players guide recommends: apply a base skin tone, add darker tones around facial features (shadows), add lighter highlights on the nose, brow, and cheekbones (where sun hits), then blend where different shades meet using a dry brush.

Painting for Distance

Parade puppet painting follows a different logic than normal painting:

  • Use high contrast. Dark eyebrows against light skin. White eye whites with dark pupils. Bright red lips. The puppet's face needs to "read" from across a street.
  • Outline everything. A thin dark outline around the eyes, nose, and mouth makes features pop at distance — like a comic book character.
  • Big, simple eyes. Large eyes are more effective than detailed eyes. Use plastic bowls, ping-pong balls, or painted circles. Strong contrast between whites, pupils, and eyelids. (My Painted Dreams)
  • Don't blend too much. Smooth gradients disappear at distance. Bold color blocks stay visible.

Step 4: Add Hair and Details

Hair, hats, crowns, and other head details come last. Keep them lightweight:

  • Felt strips — cut and staple or glue for hair, beards, or manes. Easy to work with, lightweight, comes in every color.
  • Fabric strips — for flowing hair. Staple or sew to the top and back of the head.
  • Faux fur — for animal characters. Glue with hot glue.
  • Boas — lightweight and flutter beautifully in a breeze. Good for festive characters. (Clark's guide)
  • Backer rod — foam tubing from hardware stores. Extremely lightweight. Doesn't take paint, but comes in neutral colors and can be wrapped in fabric. (Clark's guide)
Weight check. Before moving on, pick up the finished head with one hand by the mounting tube. If you can hold it overhead comfortably for 30 seconds, it's light enough. If your arm shakes, the head is too heavy. Consider removing more newspaper stuffing from inside, or thinning out heavy decorative elements. Remember: this weight will be amplified by the 3–4 foot spine lever above your head.

Check Your Understanding

What is the purpose of cutting triangular "darts" around the edge of a cardboard oval?

Darts are a technique borrowed from sewing and pattern-making. By cutting triangles from the outer edge and folding the remaining flaps inward, a flat sheet curves into a 3D bowl shape. This is the fastest way to get from flat cardboard to a curved head form.

Why should you remove newspaper stuffing from inside the head after the papier-mache dries?

The newspaper was scaffolding — it held the shape while the papier-mache dried. Once the shell is rigid, the newspaper is just dead weight. Removing it can shave significant ounces from the head, and every ounce matters at the top of a 4-foot lever arm.

Why does parade puppet painting use bold outlines and high contrast rather than smooth blending?

At parade distance (20+ feet), subtle gradients and smooth blending become invisible — everything turns to mush. High contrast, bold outlines, and big color blocks maintain their readability at distance, like a billboard or a comic book panel.
