Glossary

Quick referenceUpdated with each lesson

Structure

Access panel
A hidden opening in the puppet's costume (using velcro, ties, or overlapping fabric) that allows the puppeteer to get in and out, and to reach the frame for repairs.
Armature
The internal "support system" for papier-mache — a rough shape built from cardboard, newspaper, wire, or a bucket that you sculpt on top of before applying paper layers. Types: cardboard (beginner), clay over bucket (intermediate), clay on paper (for flat masks).
Backpack puppet
A large puppet (typically 7–12 feet tall) worn on a frame strapped to the puppeteer's back. The puppet's body towers above the puppeteer, whose movements are transmitted naturally to the puppet.
Control rod
A thin stick or dowel running from a puppet's wrist (or elbow) down to the puppeteer's hand inside the costume. Moving the rod moves the puppet's arm.
Box joint
A mounting method where a slightly oversized box or tube on the puppet head slides over the top of the spine. Allows the head to be attached and removed easily for transport.
Crossbar
A horizontal bar attached to the spine at "shoulder height," from which the puppet's arms extend outward. Typically 1/2" PVC pipe, 2–3 feet long, attached via a T-connector.
Darts
Triangular cuts made around the edge of a flat piece of cardboard. When the remaining flaps are folded inward and stapled, the flat sheet curves into a 3D bowl shape. Borrowed from sewing pattern-making.
Dresser
A helper who assists the puppeteer in putting on and taking off the backpack puppet. Most backpack puppets require a dresser.
External-frame backpack
A style of hiking pack with a rigid metal or plastic frame separate from the bag. Preferred for puppet-making because the exposed frame provides a solid attachment point for the spine.
Frame
The wearable base of a backpack puppet — typically a repurposed external-frame backpack. Transfers the puppet's weight to the puppeteer's hips and shoulders.
Hose clamp
An adjustable metal band tightened with a screwdriver. Used to attach the spine to the backpack frame. Use 2–3 spaced 8–12 inches apart.
Maquette
A small-scale model (roughly 12 inches) built quickly from scrap cardboard and tape to test a puppet's proportions and design before committing to full-size materials.
Spine (upright)
The main vertical pole rising from the frame to the head. The puppet's backbone. Made from bamboo, PVC pipe, or wooden dowel/lumber. Typically 3–4 feet of pipe rising above the frame.

Materials & Techniques

Cornstarch goop
A paste made from cornstarch and water, used as the adhesive for papier-mache. Recipe: 1 cup cornstarch + 1 cup cold water, mixed smooth, then stirred into 4 cups boiling water until thick. The standard community-puppet approach.
Cross-hatching
Alternating the direction of papier-mache strips between layers (horizontal, then vertical, then diagonal). Dramatically increases shell rigidity — the same principle as plywood.
Gesso
A white primer (acrylic-based) applied to dried papier-mache before painting. Provides a clean, neutral base and seals the surface.
Papier-mache
A construction technique where strips of paper (often brown paper bags or newspaper) are soaked in paste and layered over an armature. Dries into a lightweight, rigid shell. 2–4 layers for cardboard armatures; 4–6 for clay.
Square lashing
A knot technique using paracord or twine to bind two poles together at right angles. Used to attach bamboo poles to puppet heads or crossbars.
Staple pliers
A specialized stapler with a pointed tip that can penetrate cardboard or fabric away from the edge. The key tool for puppet cardboard construction. (Recommended: Salco P694 Sword Point.)

Types & Traditions

Bread and Puppet Theater
Founded 1963 by Peter Schumann. The seminal political puppet theater, based in Glover, Vermont. Known for giant papier-mache puppets made from river clay and recycled cardboard. The tradition that modern parade puppetry descends from.
Giant puppet
Any puppet significantly larger than the puppeteer — often 10–20+ feet tall, sometimes requiring multiple operators.
Stick puppet (parade context)
A puppet mounted on a single stick or pole held by the puppeteer, as opposed to worn on the back. Simpler than a backpack puppet but less immersive — the puppeteer is visible.

Performance

Look, Think, Move
A performance technique: before the puppet moves, let it look toward something; pause briefly (implying thought); then move. Creates the illusion of a thinking being rather than a mechanical figure.
Spotter
A companion who walks alongside the puppet to guide the puppeteer verbally, watch for hazards, and help stabilize in wind. Especially important for tall puppets or limited-visibility designs.
